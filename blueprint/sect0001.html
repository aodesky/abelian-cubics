1 Introduction

We count \(f(t)=t^3+a_1t^2+a_2t+a_3\in \mathbb {Z}[t]\) with Galois group \(C_3\), ordered by \(H(f)=\max (|a_1|,|a_2|^{1/2},|a_3|^{1/3})\). Throughout, \(E=\mathbb {Q}(\sqrt{-3})\) and \(\xi =\zeta _E\). The constants in the main terms are defined first. We write \(S=\mathbb {P}^2/C_3\), \(\mathscr {S}\) for its integral model, \(D_0\) for the image of \(X+Y+Z=0\), and \(T\) for its open torus. The integral structure and toric height are described in the chapters below.

\begin{equation*} \mu =\lim _{s\to 1} \left(\frac{\xi '(s)}{\xi (s)}+\frac{1}{s-1}\right), \quad \alpha =\prod _{p\equiv 1\pmod3}\left(1-\frac{2}{p(p+1)}\right), \quad \beta =\sum _{p\equiv 1\pmod3}\frac{(2p+1)\log p}{(p^2-1)(p+2)}. \end{equation*}
Proof in Lean ▼

The Dedekind zeta function \(\xi \) is meromorphic near \(s=1\) with a simple pole there, so \(\xi '/\xi \) has a simple pole with residue \(-1\) at \(s=1\) and the limit defining \(\mu \) exists; \(\mu \) is the Euler–Kronecker constant of \(E\). For \(p\equiv 1\pmod3\) the factor \(1-2/(p(p+1))\) lies in \((0,1)\) and differs from \(1\) by less than \(2p^{-2}\), so the product defining \(\alpha \) converges absolutely to a positive number. The terms of \(\beta \) are \(O(p^{-2}\log p)\), so the series converges absolutely.

For every \(\varepsilon {\gt}0\), as \(H\to \infty \), the number of monic integral cubic polynomials with Galois group \(C_3\) and root height at most \(H\) is

\[ H^3P(\log H)+O_\varepsilon (H^{2+\varepsilon }), \]

where

\begin{equation*} P(X)=\frac{220\alpha }{243} \biggl(X+\mu -\frac{\zeta '(2)}{\zeta (2)}+ \beta +\log 2-\frac{5}{88}\log 3 +\frac{\pi \sqrt3}{10}-\frac{17}{15}\biggr) -\frac{20\pi \sqrt3}{81}. \end{equation*}
Proof in Lean ▼

For \(f=t^3+a_1t^2+a_2t+a_3\) put \(s=-a_1\) and \(n(f)=a_1^2-3a_2\). Let \(A_1(X)\) count the polynomials \(t^3-t^2+at+b\in \mathbb {Z}[t]\) with Galois group \(C_3\) and \(1-3a\leq X\), and let \(A_0(X)\) count the polynomials \(t^3-Nt+c\in \mathbb {Z}[t]\) with Galois group \(C_3\) and \(1\leq N\leq X\). By Lemma 12, translating the roots by an integer, and also negating them when \(s\equiv -1\pmod3\), sends the polynomials of trace \(s\) bijectively to those of trace \(0\) (if \(3\mid s\)) or \(1\) (if \(3\nmid s\)) and preserves \(n(f)\), which equals \(1-3a\) in trace one and \(3N\) in trace zero. The same lemma shows that \(H(f)\leq H\) exactly when \(|s|\leq H\) and \(n(f)\leq s^2+3H^2\); the bound on \(a_3\) follows from the others by the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality. This gives the exact decomposition of Equation 37, a sum of \(A_1(3H^2+s^2)\) over \(3\nmid s\) and of \(A_0(H^2+s^2/3)\) over \(3\mid s\). Insert Equation 36 and Proposition 8, where \(Q\) is the linear polynomial of Corollary 1. The main terms become Riemann sums \(\sum _{|s|\leq H,\ s\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)}g(s/H)=\frac H3\int _{-1}^1g(u)\, du+O(1)\) for \(g(u)=3+u^2\) and \(g(u)=(3+u^2)\log (3+u^2)\), over two residue classes for \(A_1\) and one for \(A_0\). The two integrals in Equation 38 give \(P\) in the form of Equation 39, and substituting \(Q\) gives the stated \(P\). Each of the \(O(H)\) traces contributes an error \(O_\varepsilon (H^{1+\varepsilon })\) and the Riemann-sum errors contribute \(O(H^2\log H)\), so the total error is \(O_\varepsilon (H^{2+\varepsilon })\).

The proof assumes Poisson summation (Equation 15) and the Chandrasekharan–Narasimhan estimate (Cited theorem 2).

For every \(\varepsilon {\gt}0\), as \(H\to \infty \), the number of monic integral trace-one cubic polynomials with Galois group \(C_3\) and root height at most \(H\) is

\[ H^2P_1(\log H)+O_\varepsilon (H^{1+\varepsilon }) \]

where

\begin{equation*} P_1(X)=\frac\alpha 3 \biggl(X+\mu -\frac{\zeta '(2)}{\zeta (2)} + \beta +\frac78\log 3-\frac12\biggr) -\frac{\pi }{3\sqrt3}. \end{equation*}
Proof in Lean ▼

Let \(d_n\) be the number of \(P\in (\mathscr {S}\setminus \mathscr {D}_0)(\mathbb {Z})\) with \(H(P)^2=n\) (Theorem 4), and for \(f=t^3-t^2+at+b\) with Galois group \(C_3\) write \(H_{\mathrm{tor}}(f)=\sqrt{1-3a}\), the toric height of its points (Lemma 3). By Equation 33, \(\sum _{n\leq X}d_n=XQ(\tfrac 12\log X)+O_\varepsilon (X^{1/2+\varepsilon })\), where \(Q\) is the polynomial of Corollary 1; the main term is the residue of \(Z_0(z)X^z/z\) at the double pole \(z=1\) of \(Z_0(z)=\sum _{n\geq 1}d_nn^{-z}\) (Proposition 7). Lemma 10 removes the points of split polynomials, which are a third of the lattice points in an ellipse of area \(\frac{2\pi }{3\sqrt3}X\), and halves the rest, since each polynomial with Galois group \(C_3\) gives two points. This gives Equation 35. By Lemma 3, \(H(f)\leq H\) is equivalent to \(H_{\mathrm{tor}}(f)\leq \sqrt X\) with \(X=3H^2+1\). Then \(\tfrac 12\log X=\log H+\log \sqrt3+O(H^{-2})\), and since \(Q\) is linear the main term is \(\tfrac 32H^2Q(\log H+\log \sqrt3)-\frac{\pi }{3\sqrt3}H^2=H^2P_1(\log H)\), with error \(O_\varepsilon (H^{1+\varepsilon })\).

The proof assumes Poisson summation (Equation 15) and the Chandrasekharan–Narasimhan estimate (Cited theorem 2).

Theorem 3 An exact count
✓
#

Lean declarations

  • CubicTargets.Counting.ExactFormula.Claim
  • Cubic.Counting.ExactFormula.«thm:exactFormula»

For \(m\geq 1\) let \(a_E(m)\) denote the number of ideals of norm \(m\) in the ring of integers \(\mathcal{O}_E\) of \(E\). If \(t^3-t^2+at+b\in \mathbb {Z}[t]\) has Galois group \(C_3\), then \(a\leq 0\). Fix \(a \in \mathbb {Z}_{\leq 0}\) and put \(n=1-3a\). The number of polynomials of the form \(t^3 -t^2 + at + b \in \mathbb {Z}[t]\) with Galois group \(C_3\) is equal to \(\tfrac {1}{2}\big(a_E(n^3)-a_E(n)\big)\).

Proof in Lean ▼

Let \(d_n\) be the number of \(P\in (\mathscr {S}\setminus \mathscr {D}_0)(\mathbb {Z})\) with \(H(P)^2=n\) (Theorem 4), and let \(\zeta \in E\) be a primitive cube root of unity. If \(f=t^3-t^2+at+b\) has Galois group \(C_3\), its roots are normal by Lemma 9, since \(1-3a\neq 0\); so \(f\) comes from an integral point of \(T\) (Proposition 2), and Lemma 3 gives \(a\leq 0\). Fix \(a\) and \(n=1-3a\), so that \(3\nmid n\). The \(d_n\) points of height \(\sqrt n\) are two points for each such \(f\) (Equation 4) together with the split points. A split point is a triple \((x,y,z)\in \mathbb {Z}^3\) with \(x+y+z=1\) and \(xy+yz+zx=a\), up to cyclic permutation. Its element \(\theta =x+y\zeta +z\zeta ^2\in \mathcal{O}_E\) has norm \((x+y+z)^2-3(xy+yz+zx)=n\) and is congruent to \(1\) modulo \(1-\zeta \). Conversely, each \(u+v\zeta \) with \(u+v\equiv 1\pmod3\) comes from exactly one triple, and a cyclic permutation multiplies \(\theta \) by a power of \(\zeta \). An ideal of norm \(n\) has six generators \(\pm \zeta ^j\theta \), and exactly three of them are congruent to \(1\) modulo \(1-\zeta \). Hence there are \(a_E(n)\) split points, and the number of polynomials is \(\frac12(d_n-a_E(n))\). It remains to show \(d_n=a_E(n^3)\). By Remark 3, \(d_n\) is the product of \(3k+1\) over \(p^k\parallel n\) with \(p\equiv 1\pmod3\) if every prime \(q\equiv 2\pmod3\) divides \(n\) to an even power, and \(d_n=0\) otherwise. The ideal count \(n\mapsto a_E(n^3)\) is multiplicative, so it suffices to count ideals of prime-power norm. A prime \(p\equiv 1\pmod3\) splits as \(\mathfrak p\overline{\mathfrak p}\), and the ideals of norm \(p^{3k}\) are \(\mathfrak p^i\overline{\mathfrak p}^{3k-i}\) for \(0\leq i\leq 3k\), so there are \(3k+1\) of them. A prime \(q\equiv 2\pmod3\) is inert with norm \(q^2\), so there is one ideal of norm \(q^{3k}\) if \(k\) is even and none if \(k\) is odd.

The proof assumes Poisson summation (Equation 15).

Over \(\mathbb {Q}\) the surface \(S\) has the equation \(x_0(x_1^2+3x_2^2)=x_3^3\) (Equation 43). Over \(E\) it becomes the split form

\begin{equation*} x_0x_1x_2=x_3^3. \end{equation*}
Proof in Lean ▼

By Proposition 10, the coordinate change of Equation 44 identifies \(S\) with the cubic \(x_0(x_1^2+3x_2^2)=x_3^3\). Over \(E\) put \(x_1'=x_1+\sqrt{-3}\, x_2\) and \(x_2'=x_1-\sqrt{-3}\, x_2\). Then \(x_1'x_2'=x_1^2+3x_2^2\), so the equation becomes \(x_0x_1'x_2'=x_3^3\). The substitution is invertible, with \(x_1=(x_1'+x_2')/2\) and \(x_2=(x_1'-x_2')/(2\sqrt{-3})\) (Supporting lemma 1 with \(r=\sqrt{-3}\)).

The split form \(x_0x_1x_2=x_3^3\) (Equation 2) has the involution exchanging \(x_1\) and \(x_2\). For a nonsquare \(d\in \mathbb {Q}^\times \), its quadratic twist by \(\mathbb {Q}(\sqrt d)\) through this involution is

\begin{equation*} S^{(d)}\colon x_0(x_1^2-dx_2^2)=x_3^3. \end{equation*}
Proof in Lean ▼

Let \(r=\sqrt d\in \mathbb {Q}(\sqrt d)\). Over \(\mathbb {Q}(\sqrt d)\) put \(x_1'=x_1+rx_2\) and \(x_2'=x_1-rx_2\); this substitution is invertible since \(r\neq 0\) (Supporting lemma 1). Then \(x_1'x_2'=x_1^2-dx_2^2\), so \(x_0x_1'x_2'=x_3^3\) becomes \(x_0(x_1^2-dx_2^2)=x_3^3\). The nontrivial automorphism \(r\mapsto -r\) of \(\mathbb {Q}(\sqrt d)\) exchanges \(x_1'\) and \(x_2'\), which is the involution of the split form, and the split cubic is invariant under it. So \(S^{(d)}\) is the twist of the split form by \(\mathbb {Q}(\sqrt d)\) through this involution. In particular \(S=S^{(-3)}\).

Theorem 4 The one-variable height zeta function

Let \(\mathscr {D}_0=\{ X+Y+Z=0\} \subset \mathscr {S}\). The toric height functions \(H(-,s)\) of Batyrev–Tschinkel [ 2 ] are indexed by \(s\in \mathrm{Pic}(S)\otimes \mathbb {C}\). Since \(S\) has Picard rank one, we regard \(s\) as a complex number, with \(s=3\) corresponding to the ample generator. Then \(H(-,s)=H(-)^s\), where \(H(-)=H(-,1)\).

For \(\mathrm{Re}(s){\gt}2\) the series below converges absolutely, and

\begin{equation*} \sum _{P \in (\mathscr {S}\setminus \mathscr {D}_0)(\mathbb {Z})} H(P)^{-s} = \left(1-\frac{1}{3^{s/2}}\right)^2 \xi \left(\frac s2\right)^2 \prod _{q\equiv 2\pmod{3}}\left(1-\frac{1}{q^{s}}\right) \prod _{p\equiv 1\pmod{3}}\left(1-\frac{3}{p^{s}}+\frac{2}{p^{3s/2}}\right) \end{equation*}

where \(\xi \) is the Dedekind zeta function of \(E\). This height zeta function can be meromorphically continued to the half-plane \(\mathrm{Re}(s){\gt}1\) and its only pole in this region is at \(s = 2\) with order \(2\).

Proof in Lean ▼

Every \(P\in (\mathscr {S}\setminus \mathscr {D}_0)(\mathbb {Z})\) lies in the open torus \(T\): on this affine chart \(T\) is defined by \(1-3e_2\neq 0\), and \(e_2(P)\in \mathbb {Z}\) by Lemma 2. Hence the series is \(Z_0(s/2)\), where \(Z_0(z)=Z(2z,0,0)\) is the multivariate height zeta function \(Z\) of Theorem 6 on the line spanned by \(D_0\); that theorem gives absolute convergence for \(\operatorname {Re}(s){\gt}2\). Lemma 8 rewrites its Euler product as \((1-3^{-z})^2\xi (z)^2R(z)\), with \(R\) as in Equation 26, using the Euler product of \(\xi \) and the identity \((1+2x)(1-x)^2=1-3x^2+2x^3\); with \(z=s/2\) this is the displayed product. The product \(R(z)\) converges absolutely and locally uniformly for \(\operatorname {Re}(z){\gt}1/2\), which gives the continuation to \(\operatorname {Re}(s){\gt}1\). Since \(\xi \) has only a simple pole at \(1\) and \(R(1){\gt}0\), Proposition 7 shows that the only pole is a double pole at \(s=2\). Finally, \(S\) has Picard rank one [ 26 , Corollary 3.6 ] , and \(-K=D_0+D_1+D_2\) is, up to torsion in the divisor class group, the ample generator [ 26 , Theorems 3.5 and 3.7 ] , which corresponds to \(s=3\). Equation 25 shows that \(3D_0\) is linearly equivalent to \(-K\), so \(\mathcal O(D_0)\) corresponds to \(s=1\) and \(H(P)\) is the toric height \(H(P,\mathcal O(D_0))\).

The proof assumes Poisson summation (Equation 15).

For every \(\varepsilon {\gt}0\), as \(H\to \infty \),

\[ \# \{ P\in (\mathscr {S}\setminus \mathscr {D}_0)(\mathbb {Z}):H(P)\leq H\} =H^2Q(\log H)+O_\varepsilon (H^{1+\varepsilon }), \]

where

\begin{equation*} Q(X)=\frac{2\alpha }{9} \biggl(X+\mu -\frac{\zeta '(2)}{\zeta (2)} +\beta +\frac38\log 3-\frac12\biggr). \end{equation*}
Proof in Lean ▼

Let \(d_n\) be the number of \(P\in (\mathscr {S}\setminus \mathscr {D}_0)(\mathbb {Z})\) with \(H(P)^2=n\). The count is \(\sum _{n\leq H^2}d_n\), and by Theorem 4 the series \(Z_0(z)=\sum _{n\geq 1}d_nn^{-z}\) is the height zeta function at \(s=2z\). By Proposition 7, \(Z_0\) has only a double pole at \(z=1\) in \(\operatorname {Re}(z){\gt}1/2\), and its Laurent expansion there shows that the residue of \(Z_0(z)X^z/z\) at \(z=1\) is \(XQ(\tfrac 12\log X)\). Equation 33 gives \(\sum _{n\leq X}d_n=XQ(\tfrac 12\log X)+O_\varepsilon (X^{1/2+\varepsilon })\). Taking \(X=H^2\) gives \(H^2Q(\log H)+O_\varepsilon (H^{1+2\varepsilon })\), and replacing \(\varepsilon \) by \(\varepsilon /2\) proves the corollary.

The proof assumes Poisson summation (Equation 15) and the Chandrasekharan–Narasimhan estimate (Cited theorem 2).

Remark 1 The alternative ideal count
✓
#

Lean declarations

  • CubicTargets.Introduction.IdealRemark.Claim
  • Cubic.Introduction.IdealRemark.«rem:idealParametrization»

We prompted GPT-\(6.1\) with the contents of an earlier version of this paper and asked it to identify the Dirichlet series in Theorem 4. Using our Eisenstein parametrization (§4) it proved that

\[ \sum _{P \in (\mathscr {S}\setminus \mathscr {D}_0)(\mathbb {Z})} H(P)^{-s} = \sum _{\substack {0\ne J\subseteq \mathcal{O}_E\\ J+3\mathcal{O}_E=\mathcal{O}_E\\ \mathrm N J\text{ a cube}}} N^E_\mathbb {Q}(J)^{-s/6} \]

where \(J \subset \mathcal{O}_E\) runs over integral nonzero ideals. It also found a result in the literature [ 21 ] that we previously missed. After translating the \(C_3\)-polynomial counting problem into an integral Diophantine problem on \(S^{(-3)}\) (explained in the next section), Kühleitner–Nowak’s  [ 21 , Corollary 2 ] can be used to obtain an shorter proof of Corollary 1 and Theorem 2 without using the toric method, and with an error term \(O\bigl(H(\log H)^3(\log \log H)^2\bigr)\). We did not incorporate this argument as the toric approach has the merit of generalizing readily to other abelian groups where there is no analogue of [ 21 ] .

Proof in Lean ▼

Every \(P\in (\mathscr {S}\setminus \mathscr {D}_0)(\mathbb {Z})\) lies in \(T\); let \(\nu \in E^\times \) be the element attached to \(P\) by Proposition 3, so that \(\mathrm N\nu =1-3a=H(P)^2\). The element \(\theta =\mathrm N(\nu )\nu =\nu ^2\overline\nu \) has norm \(H(P)^6\). Integrality of \(P\) (Lemma 2) makes \(\theta \) an Eisenstein integer congruent to \(1\) modulo \(3\mathcal{O}_E\). Conversely, the congruences of Equation 14 recover an integral point from each such \(\theta \) of cube norm. The six units of \(\mathcal{O}_E\) map bijectively onto \((\mathcal{O}_E/3\mathcal{O}_E)^\times \), so every ideal \(J\) with \(J+3\mathcal{O}_E=\mathcal{O}_E\), equivalently \(3\nmid \mathrm NJ\), has exactly one generator congruent to \(1\) modulo \(3\mathcal{O}_E\). Hence \(P\mapsto \theta \mathcal{O}_E\) is a bijection onto the ideals in the sum, with \(\mathrm NJ=H(P)^6\), and the two series agree for \(\operatorname {Re}(s){\gt}2\). For the counts, let \(A(X)=\sum _{n\leq X}a_E(n^3)\), where \(a_E(m)\) is the number of ideals of norm \(m\). The prime above \(3\) is ramified of norm \(3\), so \(a_E((3n)^3)=a_E(n^3)\) and the sum over \(n\leq X\) with \(3\nmid n\) is \(A(X)-A(X/3)\). Apply the Kühleitner–Nowak estimate to \(A\), with \(F(s)=(s-1)^2Z_0(s)/(1-3^{-s})\), where \(Z_0(z)=\sum _{3\nmid n}a_E(n^3)n^{-z}\) is the height zeta function at \(s=2z\); the Laurent expansion in Proposition 7 gives \(F(1)\) and \(F'(1)\). The result is \(\sum _{n\leq X,\, 3\nmid n}a_E(n^3)=XQ(\tfrac 12\log X)+O(\sqrt X(\log X)^3(\log \log X)^2)\). Taking \(X=H^2\) gives Corollary 1 with error \(O(H(\log H)^3(\log \log H)^2)\). Combining this with Lemma 10 and taking \(X=3H^2+1\), as in the proof of Theorem 2, gives that theorem with the same error.

The proof assumes the Kühleitner–Nowak estimate (Cited theorem 3).

By Theorem 5, rational points of \(T\) correspond to equivalence classes of pairs \((K,x)\) of a \(C_3\)-algebra \(K\) and a trace-one normal element \(x\). Taking the characteristic polynomial of \(x\) gives a map from \(T(\mathbb {Q})\) to polynomials \(t^3-t^2+at+b\in \mathbb {Q}[t]\). Its image consists of the polynomials that have Galois group \(C_3\) or split over \(\mathbb {Q}\) with at most two equal roots. For such \(f\), the number of rational points of \(T\) above \(f\) is given below, where \((K_f,x_f)\) is any pair above \(f\) and \(\mathrm{Aut}(K_f,x_f)\) is the group of \(\mathbb {Q}\)-algebra automorphisms of \(K_f\) fixing \(x_f\).

\begin{equation*} w_f=\frac{|\mathrm{Aut}(C_3)|}{|\mathrm{Aut}(K_f,x_f)|}= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if $f$ has a double root,}\\ 2 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}\end{equation*}
Proof in Lean ▼

We count equivalence classes of pairs above \(f\). The roots \(r_1,r_2,r_3\) of \(f\) are not all equal, so \(1-3a=\frac12\sum _{i{\lt}j}(r_i-r_j)^2\neq 0\) and each root is normal by Lemma 9. If \(f\) has Galois group \(C_3\), then \(K_f=\mathbb {Q}(x_f)\) is a cyclic cubic field. The two identifications of \(C_3\) with its Galois group give inequivalent pairs (Example 1). For a fixed identification the three roots give equivalent pairs, related by Galois automorphisms. So there are two classes. If \(f\) splits, then \(K_f=\mathbb {Q}^3\) and \(x_f\) is an ordering of the roots. The \(C_3\)-equivariant automorphisms of \(\mathbb {Q}^3\) form the centralizer of \(C_3\) in \(S_3\), which is \(C_3\), so the classes are the orderings modulo cyclic permutation. The six orderings of three distinct roots form two classes, and the three orderings of a double root form one. In each case the count is \(2/|\mathrm{Aut}(K_f,x_f)|\): an automorphism fixing \(x_f\) is trivial unless \(f\) has a double root, when the transposition of the two equal coordinates also fixes \(x_f\).
